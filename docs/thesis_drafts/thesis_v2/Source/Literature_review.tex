\subsection{Simulation Fidelity}
\label{sec:lit_sim}

The fidelity of a simulation sets how far its results can be trusted on hardware. The most faithful approach reviewed is the software-in-the-loop configuration of \cite{distributedNMPC}, which runs the platform's own state estimator, control architecture and motor-controller firmware inside Gazebo, with mass-spring-damper cables. This thesis follows the same idea, with an emulated flight controller in place of the firmware, in a Gazebo twin of the rig (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). Linearised dynamics with rigid rods in MATLAB \cite{DistributedMPC} allow rapid iteration but hold only near the linearisation point and miss the nonlinear and geometric effects of aggressive manoeuvres and large attitudes. They were not adopted: Gazebo is tractable enough and keeps the full nonlinear system that the join and leave transients need.

Two trade-offs emerge. NMPC and trajectory-based methods track and handle constraints most precisely, but their cost grows with the team and real-time hardware implementation is hard. Dissipative methods are cheap, stable by energy arguments and absorb disturbances naturally, but track less precisely and cannot easily enforce hard constraints such as tension bounds or collision avoidance on their own. Changes in the team remain open: \cite{quan2025swarm} is the only reviewed work to show experimental robustness to a mid-task dropout, and few works design a protocol for adding UAVs to a team.

No existing work demonstrates a strategy for adding a UAV to an active mission mid-flight. The closest work addresses only removal \cite{tensionbased}, tolerates dropout with no mechanism for structured addition \cite{quan2025swarm}, or proposes join protocols without experimental validation or explicit force reallocation \cite{tasoojiCooperativeControlMultiQuadrotors}. None combines join and leave with a principled force allocation. This gap sets the direction of this thesis: a centralised controller that recomputes the force distribution for the new team at the moment a UAV leaves or joins (RQ1), compared against a decentralised dissipative alternative (RQ2; Section~\ref{sec:rq}). The architecture is centralised because the lab has one computer, which simplifies force allocation and the handling of joining and leaving UAVs. The dissipative alternative draws on \cite{quan2025swarm} and \cite{liuVirtualTubeBasedCooperativeTransport2026}, the trajectory-based OCP follows \cite{tudelft}, and the tension allocation of \cite{efficient} and \cite{vazquez-soquiSQPBasedCableTensionAllocation2026} informs how the central node redistributes the forces when the team changes.
