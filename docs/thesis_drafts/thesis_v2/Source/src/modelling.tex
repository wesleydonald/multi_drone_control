\subsection{Cable-Suspended Transport and Model Assumptions}
\label{sec:lit_models}

The cable connecting each UAV to the payload is a critical component of the system model, and the
assumptions made about its behaviour significantly affect both the complexity of the dynamics and
the validity of the controller. Two main modelling approaches are presented in the literature, as
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:tension}.

The most common simplification is the \textit{rigid massless rod} assumption, adopted by \cite{efficient} and \cite{wahbaKinodynamicMotionPlanning2024}, in which the cable is treated as a rigid link of fixed length $l$. The cable direction is described by a unit vector $\mathbf{q}_i$ and tension acts purely along this direction,

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{T}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{q}_i, \quad \lambda_i \geq 0
\end{equation}

where $\lambda_i$ is the scalar tension magnitude and the constraint $\lambda_i \geq 0$ is due to the taut cable assumption, as cables can pull but not push. This keeps the system state compact and computationally tractable, at the cost of being unable to capture cable slackness. \\

A more complex alternative is the \textit{mass-spring-damper chain}, used by \cite{distributedNMPC}, in which the cable is discretised into $n$ lumped masses connected by spring--damper links of stiffness $k_s$, damping $b$ and rest length $l_0$. This captures realistic cable sag and oscillation, but increases the state dimension by $6n$ per cable, which becomes complex in a multi-UAV system. \\

Regardless of the cable model used, the majority of controllers in the literature impose a \textit{taut cable assumption}, requiring cable tension to remain strictly positive throughout the task. A slack cable introduces a unilateral constraint and a discontinuity in the equations of motion, and handling this rigorously remains an open problem \cite{efficient}. For moderately aggressive indoor flight, the rigid rod model with the taut cable assumption is sufficient, consistent with \cite{tudelft}.
