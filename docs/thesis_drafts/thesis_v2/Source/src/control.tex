\subsection{Centralised Control}

Multi-UAV control architectures are classified by their information structure, which sets their scalability, robustness to communication failure and computational burden \cite{nathanReviewAutonomousMultiagent2011}. A centralised coordinator has the global state and computes every control action; distributed and decentralised approaches spread the computation across the UAVs, which rely on local communication or sensing (Figure~\ref{fig:arch_comparison}). In cable-suspended transport the payload couples the UAVs, and the coupling must either be coordinated explicitly or handled through local interactions \cite{brambillaSwarmRoboticsReview2013}.

A central node simplifies global constraints such as tension limits, inter-UAV collision avoidance and force allocation, and fits a lab where one computer commands every UAV, as in this work. Its drawbacks are a computational cost that grows with the team and a single point of failure \cite{dimakosStudyCentralisedDecentralised2021}.

\subsubsection{Trajectory-Based Planning with Receding Horizon}

Several centralised methods split the problem into a high-level trajectory planner and low-level trackers. Sun et al.\ \cite{tudelft}, the closest prior work and this thesis's main benchmark, solve a centralised receding-horizon optimal control problem online at 10\,Hz over the full coupled load--cable--UAV dynamics, with thrust limits, cable tautness and collision avoidance as path constraints, and generate trajectories for the payload and each UAV. Each UAV tracks its trajectory with an onboard incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion (INDI) controller that compensates for the cable force from inertial measurements, so the load carries no sensors (Section~\ref{sec:lit_execution}). Three and four UAVs reached speeds above 5\,m/s and accelerations above 8\,m/s\textsuperscript{2}, at least eight times the agility of prior force-based methods, including flight through narrow passages. The solve time grows exponentially with the team, although the authors expect further optimisation to scale it, and the team is fixed throughout the mission.

Wahba et al.\ \cite{wahbaKinodynamicMotionPlanning2024} plan offline: a sampling-based geometric planner finds a feasible path through clutter, and constrained nonlinear trajectory optimisation then enforces the full dynamics, actuator limits and collision avoidance. The formation may change to pass narrow gaps while the payload stays stable, but the method plans offline rather than controlling in real time.

\subsubsection{Nonlinear Model Predictive Control}

Nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC) optimises over the full nonlinear dynamics at every step and is the most constraint-rich centralised approach. Sun et al.\ \cite{sunNonlinearMPCFullPose2023} scale a centralised NMPC to ten UAVs in simulation with solve times under 100\,ms, untested on hardware. Each added UAV enlarges the state and the decision variables, so the solve time grows with the team and real-time use gets harder at scale. The NMPC of \cite{liNonlinearModelPredictive2023} controls the full pose of a rigid-body payload and is parameterised by the payload's states on $\mathrm{SE}(3)$ only, not every UAV's, which lowers the cost while keeping the coupled dynamics; the redundancy serves secondary objectives such as UAV separation and obstacle avoidance within actuator and payload limits. Simulation and experiments show accurate payload tracking at a lower computational cost than full-state NMPC.

\subsubsection{Optimisation-Based Force Allocation}

Allocation methods treat the split of a desired payload wrench into cable tensions as a separate subproblem. The work in \cite{efficient} computes the payload force and torque with geometric control on $\text{SE}(3)$ and allocates the tensions through a cascade of quadratic programs (QPs), scaling roughly quadratically rather than exponentially with the team; about ten robots are claimed and up to three flown, on rigid massless rods and taut cables. The work in \cite{vazquez-soquiSQPBasedCableTensionAllocation2026} allocates them by sequential quadratic programming (SQP), minimising the sum of squared tensions with a penalty on cable misalignment; four UAVs solve in milliseconds in simulation, not yet on hardware.

\subsubsection{Leader-Follower Control}

In leader-follower control one UAV or the payload leads and the others track offsets. Bacheti et al.\ \cite{tensionbased} make the payload the leader: the resultant cable tension is a virtual input, computed by feedback linearisation and split among the UAVs, each tracking the position and attitude that realise its share. Experiments show tracking and stable formation reconfiguration (Section~\ref{sec:lit_team}), on a point-mass payload with rigid, always-taut cables and no adaptive redistribution under uncertainty. Leader-follower schemes demand homogeneous robots and degrade sharply when the leader fails \cite{quan2025swarm}. The constrained centralised framework of \cite{9945662} enforces safety and performance constraints together with universal barrier functions and adaptive estimators for actuator saturation and inertia uncertainty; it guarantees the constraints under model uncertainty, in simulation only and for a fixed team.

\subsubsection{Limitations of Centralised Approaches}

Three limitations recur across the centralised work: a cost that grows with the team, from quadratic \cite{efficient} to exponential in NMPC and trajectory-based formulations \cite{tudelft}; a fixed team, with none handling a UAV joining mid-task and few addressing dropout beyond passive tolerance; and a single point of failure. They motivate the distributed and decentralised approaches.

\subsection{Distributed and Decentralised Control}

Distributed and decentralised architectures have no central coordinator. A decentralised UAV uses only its own measurements and does not communicate; distributed UAVs share information with their neighbours towards a joint objective \cite{nathanReviewAutonomousMultiagent2011}. Both generally tolerate faults and scale better than centralised methods, but global objectives such as precise force allocation and six-degree-of-freedom manipulation are harder to coordinate \cite{scattoliniArchitecturesDistributedHierarchical2009}.

\subsubsection{Dissipative Control}

Energy-based dissipative control is the most robust to changes in the team. In the fully decentralised controller of Quan et al.\ \cite{quan2025swarm}, inspired by table mechanics, each UAV damps the relative motion between itself and the payload from local measurements, with no communication. Any disturbance (wind, a cable perturbation, a UAV suddenly leaving) injects energy into the payload--swarm system, which damping forces proportional to the relative velocity errors remove. It is validated under varying UAV capability, cable-length uncertainty and payload weight, and depends on differential GPS, which limits it to outdoor flight. The work in \cite{liuVirtualTubeBasedCooperativeTransport2026} makes the framework distributed with a virtual tube, a corridor around the desired payload path inside which the formation adapts to avoid obstacles, with the tensions allocated by dissipative systems theory. Simulations with up to ten UAVs and outdoor experiments carry payloads through constrained spaces; real-time tube generation remains future work.

\subsubsection{Distributed Model Predictive Control}

Distributing the MPC problem reduces each UAV's load. In \cite{distributedNMPC} each UAV solves its part of the global problem, coordinated peer to peer, and experiments show centimetre-level position tracking; keeping the communication independent of team size is left open. The comparison of centralised and decentralised linear MPC for four UAVs in \cite{DistributedMPC} finds comparable tracking in simulation, with the decentralised form running faster on the same compute and the linear model limiting aggressive manoeuvres. The distributed NMPC of \cite{tasoojiEventTriggeredNonlinearModel} updates only when the system leaves its plan, enforcing robot separation and obstacle avoidance; \cite{tasoojiCooperativeControlMultiQuadrotors} adds global A* planning and decentralised communication, with updates triggered by perception such as dynamic obstacle detection, which improves responsiveness and saves computation.

\subsubsection{Sliding Mode, Adaptive, Learning and Hybrid Approaches}

The distributed fixed-time homogeneous sliding mode protocol (DFHSMC) of \cite{DFHSMC}, a leader-follower structure with a disturbance observer, limits payload swing in three-UAV experiments in wind, but inherits the leader-follower fragility and does not address team changes. The three loops of \cite{kotaruDynamicsControlQuadrotor2017} nest attitude control, sliding mode formation control on the cable load states and payload navigation, in simulation only. The leaderless distributed adaptive controller of \cite{arabPlanningDistributedControl2021} models the cable forces as an adaptive term that converges online, independent of the payload dynamics, with RRT* path planning. The work in \cite{9376103} removes external localisation with onboard monocular cameras and inertial units, fused by a distributed $\text{SE}(3)$ payload estimator over peer-to-peer links, and \cite{10144367} handles unknown payload mass and cable lengths with tension analysis and an admittance model with online estimation. Reinforcement learning has compensated for actuator saturation on top of a dynamic allocation, in simulation only \cite{liMultiUAVCooperativeTransportation2021}, and multi-agent reinforcement learning is promising but still fragmented \cite{ekechiSurveyUAVControl2025}. Hybrid approaches combine centralised planning with distributed execution: backstepping with a force distribution split into payload-position and cable-configuration layers \cite{wangRobustCooperativeTransportation2024}, a null-space formation controller for two UAVs \cite{gimenezMultiobjectiveControlCooperative2018}, and insetting-formation trajectory planning \cite{zhangIFBasedTrajectoryPlanning2023}.

\subsection{Changes in Team Composition}
\label{sec:lit_team}

Most of the work above assumes a fixed team \cite{efficient, wahbaKinodynamicMotionPlanning2024, quan2025swarm}; a few works let the team or its formation change during a mission, and none adds a UAV to a carried payload. Quan et al.\ \cite{quan2025swarm} show experimentally that when a UAV drops out the others self-organise and continue the trajectory with little payload swing and no replanning, a robustness rare in experiments. It does not extend to addition: a joining UAV receives no information about force sharing or its attachment. Bacheti et al.\ \cite{tensionbased} handle removal explicitly: before a UAV releases, the others reposition to minimise the transient, and the same reconfiguration contracts and restores the formation for narrow corridors in experiments. It changes the formation, not the team, as does the offline planner of \cite{wahbaKinodynamicMotionPlanning2024}, but the idea could serve the addition or removal of UAVs. For joining, the event-triggered decentralised architecture of Tasooji et al.\ \cite{tasoojiCooperativeControlMultiQuadrotors} suggests better scalability through less communication and computation, but varying team sizes are not analysed and the join protocol has neither experimental validation nor explicit force reallocation. None of these recomputes the force allocation for the new team at the moment a UAV leaves or joins.

\subsection{Execution Layer and Steady-Error Rejection}
\label{sec:lit_execution}

An execution layer sits between the planner's commands and each UAV's actuation. In Sun et al.\ \cite{tudelft} an onboard INDI controller \cite{smeur2016indi} turns the planned trajectories into motor commands and compensates for the UAV's uncertain dynamics and the cable force without a disturbance model: centralised planning paired with robust local execution, the fast dynamics handled onboard and the slow coordination centrally. Without such a layer, a constant disturbance or model error leaves a steady error in what the planner controls. Offset-free MPC removes it by adding a constant disturbance to the prediction model and estimating it from the measurements \cite{muske2002disturbance, pannocchia2003disturbance, maeder2009linear}. By the internal model principle \cite{francis1976internal}, a constant disturbance is then rejected with no steady error. The disturbance can be modelled at the input or at the output, which changes the transient behaviour, not the steady state \cite{pannocchia2007combined}. Section~\ref{sec:disturbance} builds the planner's integral action on this, and Section~\ref{sec:res_steady} compares it with estimators from the literature.
