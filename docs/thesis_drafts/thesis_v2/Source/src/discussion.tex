\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% 9 Oct (structure audit): the list of simulator artefacts moved here from the experimental method;
% the steady-error taxonomy points at Table tab:error_sources.

This section discusses the answers in question order, then the role of geometry, the twin against the
rig, the literature and the limitations.

\paragraph{Q1: carrying with a fixed team.} The cascade carries the ring with all control on the ground and
the UAVs' firmware unchanged: a planner that re-solves the coupled payload--rod problem at 10\,Hz and trackers
that send rate commands over the radio are enough at lab speeds. What limited the carry on the rig was not the
plan but what the trackers were told about the rods. The plan assumes massless rods at their planned angle;
the real rods flew steeper, so the planned pull was wrong, and the ring lagged its path and its height swung
until each tracker used the pull it measures and the rods were planned at 60$^\circ$
(Table~\ref{tab:rig_oct7}). Measuring the rod's pull at each UAV is the ground-side counterpart of the onboard
force inversion of \cite{tudelft}, and it lets a plan made centrally hold on hardware that the planner does
not model exactly.

\paragraph{Q2: steady errors in a re-planned cascade.} Re-solving from the measured state at every tick closes a
loop on the payload's position with no integrator, of stiffness about 5\,N/m horizontally and 7\,N/m vertically
in the twin, so a force of half a newton moves the payload by 7--10\,cm and stays. The payload's offsets had four
kinds of cause (Table~\ref{tab:error_sources}): a bias in the cascade itself (the tracker's relaxed initial
condition, which no force estimate could find because it is not a force), a wrong belief passed between levels
(the breakaway cap), model errors (the rods' mass, one UAV's thrust map) and external forces (a push, wind, the
rig's steady pull towards $-x$). Integral action removes the steady error from all of them, as the internal
model principle says, but only where its gate is open and within its bound; whether it moves the target or acts
as a force in the planner's model changes the transient and what the plan predicts, not the steady state. A
force estimated from motion and thrust removes external forces quickly but, by Newton's law, is blind to forces
the system exerts on itself, and only a correction of one UAV's own force removes the moment of its wrong thrust
map. In \cite{tudelft} the onboard incremental inversion makes each UAV exact at its own force, at 300\,Hz from
rotor speed, which is why the planner needs no integrator there. With Betaflight and a ground-side controller the
equivalent is the tracker's measured rod pull: in the twin's estimator study it levelled the ring against a wrong
thrust map but upset the landings, and on the rig, flown since 7 October, it removed most of the force the
model integral had been learning (Section~\ref{sec:res_rig_integral}). Integral action in the planner then
removes what remains.

\paragraph{RQ1a: leave.} In simulation the answer is yes, with a condition: a detach by OCP resize
redistributes the load in the first solve after the release, and the payload settles level within a few
seconds, provided the survivors' attachment points still surround its centre of mass. The centralised planner
knows the whole attachment geometry, so it does not have to discover the new load distribution from the motion
that follows the event: it solves for it. This supports the hypothesis of the project plan. On the rig the
detach held the ring in all four flights, announced and unannounced, in a hover and in the circle, with peak
tilts of 6.5--17.8$^\circ$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_detach_rig}). That an unannounced detach, found from the
stretched rod 0.33--0.42\,s after the release, left the ring no more tilted than an announced one suggests that
the planner need not be told of a loss in advance, on one run per condition.

\paragraph{RQ1b: join.} The attach has been flown only in the rig twin, and its weld transient is not yet
measured against its bar (Section~\ref{sec:sim_attach}). What the twin does show is that the parts around the
weld work: the newcomer's measured-tip loop removes the error of an assumed rod length, and the fleet lands
from every phase of an attach, except in some runs within 2\,s of the weld, where the twin's rigid rod, which
the rig's tether does not have, tips the newcomer.

% --- original text, flown before the rig twin; kept to refill this place:
% \paragraph{RQ2: trajectory-based against dissipative.} On this system the trajectory-based planner was
% better in every test: tracking, detach and attach. The dissipative network did as well as the
% literature suggests only once its tracker was replaced with the velocity loop it assumes, and even then
% it was not robust to a heavier payload. Its strengths are real but did not apply here. With rigid rods
% the payload is held by geometry, which the network can only approximate. Its dissipation acts on
% virtual nodes rather than the payload. And it cannot use the measured payload pose without
% destabilising itself. A combination remains possible, with the planner for tracking and
% reconfiguration and a compliant layer at the weld, but with the OCP resize the evidence does not call
% for it.

\redo{the geometry cases behind this paragraph: three UAVs at 3, 12 and 9 o'clock hanging the ring tilted, the even ring of four after a detach, the ring on two rim points, and the uneven layout's load share turning into a roll (Sections~\ref{sec:sim_detach} and~\ref{sec:sim_attach}).}
% --- original text, flown before the rig twin; kept to refill this place:
% \paragraph{Geometry decides what control can do.} Several results that first looked like control
% problems were geometry. Three UAVs at 3, 12 and 9 o'clock hang the ring tilted, because its centre of
% mass lies on the edge of their support triangle. An even ring of four loses its support after one
% UAV leaves. A ring on two rim points is a pendulum. An uneven layout puts 40\,\% of the load on one
% UAV, which a tracker without an integrator turns into a roll. Choosing the attachment layout for the
% reconfigurations that will happen is part of the design, and a simple test (is the centre of mass
% inside the support polygon, and by how much) predicts which layouts will work.

\paragraph{Simulation and the rig.} The rig twin, flown on exactly the rig's code, matched the rig in the
takeoff and in solver health, and reproduced the rig's height shortfall from the one setting that caused it
(Section~\ref{sec:sim_to_real}). It did not reproduce the slower breakaway, the larger tilt near the floor or
the steady pull towards $-x$, suspected to be an effect near the floor, the downwash, and the room or its
calibration; none of them is modelled. Many apparent controller faults in simulation were simulator artefacts
that had to be found and removed before the twin could be trusted: rate estimates differenced from unstamped
poses turned bunched samples into rate spikes under CPU load (the emulated rate loop now uses the gyro); a
proportional-only emulated rate loop could not hold the moment of a rod pivoting below a UAV's centre and
flipped UAVs when control was handed over; the physics engine ignores damping on ball joints, so a damped rod
needs universal joints; and the wall-clock watchdogs fire when the simulation runs slower than real time
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation}). Finding them took as long as the controller work.

\paragraph{Against the literature.} Table~\ref{tab:lit_compare} sets this work against the closest works,
in the numbers each reports. On a path the rig's ring stayed within 3\,cm of its reference on average, 3--6\,\%
of the path's half-extent, the same order as Sun et al.'s 4.1\,\% at 1\,m/s and Wang et al.'s 4.7\,\%; but this
work flew at a tenth of their speed, so the comparison places its accuracy at low speed only. No other work in
the table reports the transient of a UAV leaving the team in flight: Quan et al.\ remove or lose a UAV with
94\,\% success but give no transient, and none flies a UAV joining. The rig's detach, with peak tilts of
6.5--17.8$^\circ$ and dips of 5--7\,cm, is the only such number in the table; the attach has yet to give its rig
number.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The work is at lab scale (a 0.86\,kg payload, two to four UAVs, a
6\,$\times$\,6\,m room) with motion capture as the only sensing and all computation on one ground
computer. The rods are rigid, so slack cables are outside the model. The attach depends on an
electromagnet and on a collaborator's approach controller. The hardware results cover carrying with
