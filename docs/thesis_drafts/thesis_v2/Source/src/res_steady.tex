\subsection{Steady payload error}
\label{sec:res_steady}

This section answers Q2: what leaves a steady error in the payload's position, and what removes it?
Each source below is set against the same flight without its remedy, which is its baseline: the tracker's
relaxed initial condition, the breakaway cap held for the whole flight, and no integral action for forces
and wrong model values. The bar is the ring back within 1\,cm of its target, the band used for the
recovery times below.

\subsubsection{The tracker's initial condition}
\label{sec:res_ic}

An earlier version of the tracker relaxed its initial condition to a box with $\epsilon = 0.025$
(Section~\ref{sec:tracker}). Figure~\ref{fig:tracker_x0} compares it with node~0 pinned to the
measurement ($\epsilon = 0$), both without integral action (means in Table~\ref{tab:x0},
Appendix~\ref{sec:app_tests}).

With the box, each tracker could start its prediction from any state within 2.5\,\% of the
measured one, about 3.5\,cm of height at 1.4\,m, so the box acted as a dead band. The solver used that
freedom to treat its UAV as closer to the reference than it was, so small errors were not corrected:
the formation came to rest at the box's edge, and the payload held about 5\,cm above its target in
simulation. Pinning node~0 brings the offset to under 1\,cm without any integral action.

The box was a fraction of each \emph{absolute} coordinate, so it widened as the UAVs moved away from
the world origin. The circle's error with the box grows with the ring's distance from the origin,
which is consistent with this (Figure~\ref{fig:tracker_x0}b), although on this circle the distance and
the phase of the lap change together. Pinning node~0 also removes most of that error, and with four UAVs
it cut the horizontal error on the circle and the figure-8 by about a factor of three
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app_tests}). The landings that tipped a UAV, with and without the box, came from the
landing, not from the box.


\subsubsection{The breakaway cap}
\label{sec:res_cap}

On the rig on 1 October (Table~\ref{tab:rig_oct1}), without the height integral the ring held 5\,cm low at 0.5\,m, and at
higher targets it sat 20--30\,cm low before the integral took over, which then saturated at its bound.
The figure-eight, where the integral does not run, held the ring 29\,cm low. The shortfall was largest
on the flights whose ring broke free at the smallest fraction of the planned pull. The cause was
found afterwards in the logs: the breakaway freeze had held the pull at that fraction for the whole
flight, so the trackers were told the rods pull 7--27\,\% less than they do. Holding the pull at
75\,\% in the Gazebo twin reproduced both symptoms on the rig: the ring 23--26\,cm low and the rods
steepened from 52 to 58$^\circ$. Releasing the cap once the lift starts restored the twin's height
to the no-cap value (Section~\ref{sec:sim_to_real}). On the rig the release has flown since 7 October,
together with the pinned initial condition: without an integral the ring then held 0.97\,m against its 1.0\,m target before it sank to 0.92\,m
(Figure~\ref{fig:rig_integral}), where with the cap held it had sat 20--30\,cm low.

\subsubsection{External forces}
\label{sec:res_force}

A constant force was applied to the ring for 15\,s in hover: 0.6\,N downward, about the weight of a
60\,g object, and 0.4\,N horizontally. Both were sized so that the integral stays inside its
bound, at 56\,\% and 51\,\% of it. Without an integral the ring settles 8.4\,cm and 7.6\,cm away, which gives the closed loop's
stiffness to a force on the ring: $K_z = 7.1$\,N/m and $K_{xy} = 5.3$\,N/m, the values used for
$K$ in \eqref{eq:lumped_int}. Figure~\ref{fig:disturbance} shows the model integral on the horizontal push, and
Table~\ref{tab:disturbance} gives both pushes and compares it with the target-shift form.

Both forms bring the ring back to the target, with vertical transients the same to within a few per
cent, since with $K$ set to the measured stiffness the loop has the same gain either way. The model
integral learns the applied force within 3\,\% vertically and 4--5\,\% horizontally, and only it acts
horizontally, removing the horizontal push in the same way.

The integral reduces the peak only partly, by about a quarter vertically (8.9 to 6.5\,cm) and by 7\,\%
horizontally (7.7 to 7.2\,cm), because it is slower than the cascade: the ring reaches most of its
uncompensated displacement within about 1\,s, against the integral's time constant of
$1/k_i = 2.5$\,s. When the push ends, the integral still holds the learned force and drives the ring past
the target by about as much as the push displaced it at onset, an overshoot on release that is a property
of integral action, not of where the integral acts. Doubling $k_i$ reduces the peak by
14\,\%, but the response rings and settles 55\,\% later (one run; Table~\ref{tab:gain},
Appendix~\ref{sec:app_tests}), the trend the damping relation of Section~\ref{sec:disturbance} predicts
(with $\tau \approx 0.9$\,s, measured vertically, and $k_i = 0.4$\,s$^{-1}$, $\zeta \approx 0.84$).

When one UAV produces more thrust than its map assumes, both forms hold the height. Only the model
form also removes the horizontal error, and the ring tilts 1.5$^\circ$ further. The thrust error is a
moment on the ring that neither form controls.

\paragraph{The rig's pull towards $-x$.} In every rig flight of 1 October (Table~\ref{tab:rig_oct1}) the ring
sat 3--17\,cm from its horizontal reference, always towards the room's $-x$ direction, and nothing in the
controller pulled it back. The offset kept its direction when the whole system was rotated by 124$^\circ$,
so it does not come from a particular UAV, rod or the ring. Estimated offline from the UAVs' delivered thrust
(the identified map and their measured attitudes) less the total weight, the force behind it was
0.41--0.52\,N towards $-x$ over the steady holds, about 1$^\circ$ of the fleet's thrust, which at the
planner's horizontal stiffness of about 5\,N/m explains offsets of 8--10\,cm. Moving the system 1.1\,m
towards $+x$ reduced the offset to 3--5\,cm, in a single flight. Two explanations remain open: a
motion-capture ground plane tilted by about 1$^\circ$, which would put a component of gravity along $x$ in
the controller's frame, or air moving in the room; a plumb-line check of the calibration comes first at the
next session.

\subsubsection{On the rig: the model integral and the measured rod pull}
\label{sec:res_rig_integral}

On 7 October the rig flew three hovers at 1.0\,m with four UAVs, changing one thing at a time
(Figure~\ref{fig:rig_integral}). Without an integral the ring sank in the 4\,s it held before LAND. The model
integral brought it back to its target by learning a downward force of up to 0.9\,N, but the ring then bobbed
at about 0.2\,Hz. With the trackers' measured rod pull added (Section~\ref{sec:tracker}) it held within 2\,cm
and the integral learned only 0.1\,N: most of the missing force was in the rods' pull, which the planned pull
got wrong. On the circle and the figure-eight, the measured pull with the rods planned at 60$^\circ$ cut the
standard deviation of the ring's height from 4.8 and 4.7\,cm to 0.5 and 0.6\,cm (Table~\ref{tab:rig_oct7}).
Each is one run; the measured-pull hover held for 7\,s, ending before the first swing of the integral-only
run, so the circle and the figure-eight, flown for 36 and 68\,s, carry the claim that the swing is gone.

\subsubsection{Wrong model values}
\label{sec:sim_robustness}

The controller is given the payload's mass, the rod lengths, the attachment radius and each UAV's thrust
map; on the rig each is measured, and each can be wrong. In the rig twin's hover at 1.0\,m with one wrong
value (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_robust}, Table~\ref{tab:robust}), a wrong payload mass moves the hover by about
1.25\,cm per percent of mass, which the height integral removes while the miss is inside its 0.25\,m gate; a
20\,\% overestimate puts the ring just over the gate, so the integral never starts and the ring holds 26\,cm
high. Measuring the rods at the hand-over absorbs a 5\,cm rod error; without it the ring sits 8\,cm high and
the integral, waiting for every rod to read taut, does not help. A 2\,cm error in the attachment radius has
no visible effect, and all 28 flights landed.

Before the initial-condition fix, the alternatives of Section~\ref{sec:disturbance} were flown in the
three-UAV rig twin against the hover, one UAV's thrust map wrong in either direction, a 1\,N push and a
0.4\,m/s wind (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_methods}, Table~\ref{tab:methods}). Two conclusions carried forward.
First, the internal height offset was not a force at all, which is why no force estimate could find it; it
turned out to be the tracker's initial condition. Second, a wrong thrust map on one UAV is a moment on the
ring, and only a correction of that UAV's own force (the measured cable force) removes the tilt it causes;
a force on the ring, estimated or integrated, can only move the whole formation.

\subsubsection{Tilt and height}
\label{sec:res_tilt}

On the rig the ring's tilt fell with its height. Over the first three flights of
Table~\ref{tab:rig_oct1} the ring's mean tilt fell from
5.0$^\circ$ at 0.45\,m to 2.5$^\circ$ at 0.67\,m and 1.6$^\circ$ at 0.94\,m; the later hovers at
0.9--1.0\,m had 2.2--3.8$^\circ$. Close to the floor the ring sits in the UAVs' downwash and its
recirculation off the floor, and flying higher was the most effective single change for a steady
ring.

\paragraph{Answer to Q2.} Four kinds of source leave a steady payload error, a bias in the cascade (the
tracker's relaxed initial condition), a wrong belief passed between levels (the breakaway cap), model
errors (the rods' mass, the payload's mass, one UAV's thrust map) and external forces, and each needs its
own remedy (Table~\ref{tab:error_sources}): pinning node~0 and releasing the cap remove the first two at
their source, integral action in the planner's model removes a force on the payload in all three axes
within its gate and bound, and only a correction of one UAV's own force removes the moment of its wrong
thrust map.
